Question for today: CellCo’s cell line is running, slow or down in every shift. How much of the time will it run, and is the night shift really worse?

A line that changes state

Every shift, CellCo records the state of the cell line: Running (R), Slow (S) or Down (D).

A Markov chain assumes the next state depends only on the current state, not on the history before it.

The transition matrix P holds the probability of moving from each state (row) to each state (column). Each row sums to 1.

After about four shifts the line forgets where it started. In the long run it runs 60% of shifts. If a slow shift makes half the cells, throughput is 0.6 + 0.5 × 0.2 = 70% of capacity.

Watch the chain forget

Two quick readings of π

Mean recurrence time. The line returns to Down every 1/πD = 1/0.2 = 5 shifts on average.

Mean run length. Once running, it stays running for 1/(1 − 4/6) = 3 shifts on average.

Capacity planning. At 70% effective throughput, a line rated 2 GWh/yr delivers about 1.4 GWh/yr. This links directly to the demand numbers in Sessions 1 to 3.

Activity 1 · Dice Markov chain

Whole class, then pairs · 25 minutes · one die each

You are the cell line. Start in Running and roll your die ten times, writing down your state after each roll.

  1. From R: 1 to 4 → R, 5 → S, 6 → D. From S: 1 to 3 → R, 4 and 5 → S, 6 → D. From D: 1 to 3 → R, 4 → S, 5 and 6 → D.
  2. Count your transitions (R→R, R→S, …) and add them to the table on the board.
  3. In pairs, estimate each transition probability from the class total. How close is it to the true sixths?
  4. What share of all recorded shifts was Running? Compare with 0.6.

Studying alone: roll 30 times and estimate the Running row.

Estimating P from a log

p̂ij = nij / ni     SE = √( p̂ij(1 − p̂ij) / ni )     95% interval ≈ p̂ ± 1.96 SE

Count the moves out of each state, then divide by the row total. This is the maximum-likelihood estimate.

The day shift’s log has 635 moves out of Running, 448 of them to Running: p̂RR = 0.706, SE 0.018, so the 95% interval is 0.67 to 0.74.

A row with few visits has a wide interval. Down was visited only 189 times by day, so its row has an SE of about 0.035, twice that of the Running row.

For each row, a chi-square test compares day and night (2 × 3 table, 2 degrees of freedom). The three tests add up:

χ² = 16.85 + 1.46 + 2.24 = 20.55, df = 6, p = 0.002.

One matrix does not fit both shifts. The difference sits in the Running row: by night, a running line breaks down in 23% of shifts, against 15% by day.

Day: p̂RD = 95/635 = 0.150 ± 0.028. Night: 122/528 = 0.231 ± 0.036. The intervals do not overlap.

Steady-state share of Running: day matrix 0.635, night matrix 0.528. Matching the day shift would add about 107 running shifts per 1,000.

Investigate why running night shifts break down: maintenance handover, staffing, or the start-up after a planned stop. The Slow and Down rows do not differ significantly.

What we learned

A Markov chain is a transition matrix. πn = π0Pn, and the steady state solves π = πP. CellCo’s line runs 60% of shifts in the long run.

Estimate each row as counts divided by visits, and report its interval. Rarely visited states have the widest intervals.

Test whether one matrix fits all conditions before you use it. CellCo’s night shift breaks down more often, χ² = 20.6 with p = 0.002.
