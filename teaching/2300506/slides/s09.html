When resources are limited

F (install containers, 2.0 weeks) and G (install power conversion, 3.17 weeks) both need CellCo’s only specialist installation crew.

In the CPM schedule they overlap: G runs from week 14.83 to 18.00, F from 16.50 to 18.50. With one crew, one must wait for the other.

Activity · Answer

G is ready first, at week 14.83 (after E). F is ready at 16.50 (after C).

“G first” finishes at 22.17 weeks; “F first” at 23.83. Starting whichever job is ready first wins here by 1.67 weeks.

A second crew restores the 20.67-week CPM plan, 1.5 weeks earlier than 22.17. That is worth 1.5 × 0.5 = CNY 0.75 million against a cost of 0.6, so hire it.

Shortest path: routing a delivery

Dijkstra’s algorithm labels each node with its shortest known time from P, always fixing the unvisited node with the smallest label next.

The shortest route is P-a-c-T, 95 minutes. Going to the nearest junction first (b, 25 minutes) leads to at best 100 minutes: nearest-first is not shortest.

Maximal flow: the line’s bottleneck

LinkCapacity, cells/h
Source → formation F1, F2900, 600
F1 → aging A1, A2500, 400
F2 → aging A1, A2300, 300
A1 → packing, A2 → packing700, 600
Packing → shipping1,400

The maximal flow is 1,300 cells/h. It equals the capacity of the minimum cut, the two links from aging to packing: 700 + 600.

Adding formation capacity buys nothing: raising F1’s supply to 1,200 still gives 1,300. Raising A2 → packing to 800 lifts the flow to 1,400, where packing becomes the limit.

Max-flow min-cut theorem: the largest flow through a network equals the smallest total capacity of links whose removal disconnects source from sink.

What we learned

A shared resource can lengthen a project beyond its critical path. With one crew, CellCo’s plan grows from 20.67 to 22.17 weeks.

Dijkstra’s algorithm finds shortest routes; going to the nearest node first can miss them.

The maximal flow equals the minimum cut. Invest in the links of the cut, not elsewhere.
